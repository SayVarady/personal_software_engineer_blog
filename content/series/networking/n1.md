## A Name Is Not an Address

Nothing on the internet is reached by name. `example.com` means nothing to a router — routers move packets toward IP addresses, numbers like `93.184.216.34`. So why do we use names at all? The name is a convenience for humans than to remember the series of numbers like IP addresses. The "example.com" is resolved to an address (like `93.184.216.34`) before anything else can happen, it called DNS resolution. It's worth understanding as a real lookup, not a black box.

DNS is a distributed, hierarchical directory. Your machine asks a resolver (often your ISP's, or a public one like 1.1.1.1); that resolver asks a root server which zone owns `.com`; the `.com` server says who owns `example.com`; that nameserver finally answers with the IP. Every layer can cache its answer for a while — that's what a TTL (time-to-live) controls. This is exactly why "I updated my DNS record and nothing changed" is normal: somewhere between you and the browser, a cached answer is still valid by its own clock.

## An Address Gets You to a Machine, Not a Program

An IP address identifies a network interface — a machine, roughly. But a machine runs many programs at once: a web server, a database, an SSH daemon. The thing that tells the network stack which program on that machine should receive the data is the **port**. `93.184.216.34:443` is "this machine, whichever process is listening on port 443" — by convention, HTTPS. `:22` is SSH. `:5432` is often Postgres. The IP gets the packet to the right doorstep; the port gets it to the right room.

This is why `curl localhost:3000` and `curl localhost:8080` can return completely different applications on the very same machine, and why "connection refused" versus "timeout" are different failures: refused means a machine answered but nothing is listening on that port; timeout usually means nothing answered at all — often a firewall silently dropping the packet, or the wrong address entirely.

## Every Hop Is a Decision

Between your laptop and that server sit routers — machines whose entire job is looking at a packet's destination IP and deciding which direction to forward it, one hop at a time, based on routing tables they maintain. Your laptop doesn't know the whole path; each router only knows "for this range of addresses, send it that way." `traceroute` makes this visible: it shows you every hop the packet took, and it's usually the fastest way to tell "is this slow because of my wifi, my ISP, or the server" apart.

Inside your own home or office network, addresses are usually private (`192.168.x.x`, `10.x.x.x`) and don't exist on the public internet at all. Your router does **NAT** (network address translation) — rewriting your private address to its one public address on the way out, and remembering how to route the reply back to you. That's the mechanism behind "why can't I reach my laptop from outside my house without extra configuration": it doesn't have a public address to begin with.

## Key Takeaways

- Names are resolved to IP addresses via DNS before anything else happens; caching (TTL) is why DNS changes don't propagate instantly.
- An IP address identifies a machine; a port identifies which program on that machine should get the data.
- Packets travel hop-by-hop through routers, each making a local forwarding decision — `traceroute` makes this path visible.
- Private networks use NAT to share one public address, which is why machines behind a home router aren't directly reachable from outside by default.
